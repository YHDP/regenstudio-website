A furniture maker who builds every piece once faces the same European rules as a factory that makes the same cabinet a thousand times. There is no exemption by company size in the chemicals regulation REACH or in the deforestation regulation (EUDR), and we do not expect one for the Digital Product Passport (DPP) under the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR). In 2026 Regen Studio and Meubelmakerij Houtgoed in Rotterdam looked together at what those rules mean for a small workshop where every piece of furniture is a one-off design.

We did it in four steps. First we mapped what the different rules could ask for between them, then we designed a complete system from data entry through to the published passport, next we built that system so the maker fills it in while he works, and finally we tried it on a real piece of furniture: the Stadstoel, a rocking chair of oak from Rotterdam's city trees with a strip of teak from an old floor. Its passport is online. Below we describe what Regen Studio did in the pilot and the lessons we drew from it, which we also set down in a white paper.

First, find out what could be asked

The rules for furniture do not exist yet. The European Commission's working plan names 2028 for the delegated act under the ESPR, and the passport obligation usually starts eighteen months later, so probably not before the end of 2029. Waiting for that text seemed the wrong order to us, because a small workshop does not overhaul its records in a few months. We read seventy sources on the regimes that can touch a wooden piece of furniture, from legal texts and harmonised standards to European guidance and studies by industry bodies, and gathered everything those rules could ask for into one data overview.

That produced 218 data points, a data point being one separate item of information, such as the wood species or the weight of a part. Of these, 52 are already set in the EUDR or REACH, 67 we expect from the ESPR and the European passport standards, and 99 are voluntary, such as a buy-back offer. 129 points appear in the passport, while the other 89 exist only in the maker's own records: purchasing, stock, offcuts, margin.

Read that number as an exploration. The 218 adds up what the different rules could ask for between them and what a maker can choose to share; it is not an estimate of what the furniture delegated act will actually require, which may ask far less, or something else.

      Two laws come first

      While the sector waits for the furniture rules, two other laws already apply or soon will. Since 6 August 2026 a piece of furniture may not release more than 0.062 mg/m³ of formaldehyde, unless the formaldehyde occurs only naturally in the wood. From 30 December 2026 a wooden piece of furniture falls under the EUDR. The delay to 30 June 2027 applies only to small businesses that were already established before 2025, and not to tables and cabinets; Houtgoed was founded later and gets no delay.

      Both laws enter the workshop at purchasing, and that is where most of the administrative gain lies. One board without coverage makes the maker an operator for the whole piece, with the result that he must be able to supply the geolocation of the plots. One material with added formaldehyde brings the whole piece under the emission limit. Choose wood with coverage, and glue, board and finish without added formaldehyde, and a yes or no from the supplier is enough instead of a test report. So we wrote a standard supplier request: per material, one question about formaldehyde and substances on the Candidate List, and per batch of wood, a question about the supplier's EUDR role and reference number.

      Practice lags behind the law. None of the workshop's seven supplier documents named a due diligence statement reference number in October 2026, and the wood supplier of the Stadstoel did not know the EUDR was coming. With the city wood, the maker received only the place name Rotterdam, while the felling decision that shows the tree was cut legally does not travel with the board. On 5 November 2026 we therefore asked the municipality of Rotterdam, under the Dutch Open Government Act, for the felling decisions for felled tree-of-heaven and oak trees. Our advice to municipalities is to publish the location, the felling date and the decision for each felled tree, so a passport can link to it.

      Tools that follow the working day

      A ready-made open passport system for a small maker does not exist. The complete platforms we looked at fell away on their licence or on an architecture that assumes a cluster, its own login service or a data space, and none of the purchasing and stock packages we examined makes a passport. So we built a light system with ten parts: seven on the maker's computer, three on his own website.

      The system follows six phases, and five of them are already part of the working day: intake, design, purchasing, making and delivery. Publishing is new. The maker drags an invoice into the system, which reads the supplier, the items and the quantities and links the batch to the piece of furniture. The invoice is the last place where those details are fixed, so that is where you have to catch them. The invoice reader works only with a profile per supplier: without a profile it read not a single line correctly, with a profile per invoice layout it read 95 out of 95. Because the system replaces the purchase list and the stock list, one entry gives the margin, the stock and the passport.

      Two rules keep it honest. Unknown can always be chosen, with a flag that stays visible until the information is filled in, and missing information never silently becomes zero. On top of that, every field has a legal basis and an access level: public, on request, authorities or internal. Purchase price, margin and supplier addresses never go into the passport.

      The 3D model helps fill it in

      The workshop designs every piece in 3D, and that three-dimensional design file turned out to be the best filling aid. The STEP export yields seven data points, followed by the derived fields that take the most work by hand: weight, connectors and an indication of formaldehyde release. Sawing waste remained manual work. It works only with materials from a library, because a STEP file carries only names and structure. Every weight gets a flag (from the model, from a consumption norm, or estimated), otherwise an estimate looks like a measurement.

      There was no shared vocabulary for joints between parts, so we set one up with ten types, from glued and lamello to cam lock and clamp connector. The type of joint tells whether a part comes apart: a cam lock comes apart without breaking, a lamello is a glued wooden biscuit and therefore fixed. That way the system derives what a repairer needs to know, without the maker filling in an extra field. The 3D model itself is business capital, so the maker decides per piece whether it goes public.

      A passport on the maker's own website

      The passport of the Stadstoel lives at houtgoed.nl/p/atcrzjn4, with no service provider in between. It is a static file on the maker's website, under a product code he issues himself: did:web, a form the standard EN 18219 allows. Every version is kept separately, and the QR code on the wooden plate points to a redirect, so the passport can move while the engraved code stays the same.

      So self-hosting works, and the maker may also register in the European registry himself. A small maker cannot meet the requirements for a protected part, a server that checks tokens from the registry. A passport with only a public part would be considerably simpler. Practical details also took unexpected time: the first engraving file for the plate consisted of thousands of loose strips that the laser software could not fill.

      What we learned

      The maker spent more than an hour on one chair of three wood species and about ten boards, and afterwards was still not convinced he had filled in every field in a legally valid way. That is a lot. The design aimed at a quarter of an hour per piece. Whether that target was well chosen only a second piece can show, because the Stadstoel was the first in an empty system.

      Our position is a lighter passport obligation for small makers of custom furniture: fewer data fields, simpler technology and a passport the maker can host himself. The ESPR itself asks that a requirement does not impose a disproportionate administrative burden, especially on micro-enterprises. The strongest argument against it is that passports become less comparable. And the line between custom work and small series is vague. Our view changes if the second piece goes into the system much faster than the first. If the simplification does not come, invest in 3D skills and in shared libraries of materials and parts. Without a library, a 3D model gives only dimensions.

      One lesson went against expectations. A good glued joint of wood on wood is stronger than the wood and lasts longer, while a disassembly requirement can reward loose joints instead. That is a claim from the workshop, not yet tested, but because the ESPR names disassembly as a product parameter, a furniture delegated act can ask for it.

      All lessons, the assumptions and sixteen recommendations for makers, manufacturers, trade associations and policymakers are in the white paper Richting een Meubelpaspoort (towards a furniture passport), which Regen Studio published with Houtgoed in November 2026.
