The project DPP-readiness voor wol half-fabrikaten (KVW3-00583) is co-funded by the European Union through the European Regional Development Fund (ERDF), the Province of South Holland and Kansen voor West III.

What does the European Digital Product Passport (DPP) mean for a small wool supplier? That question drove a 2026 project by Regen Studio and CircularTech Forum with Hollands Wol Collectief, a Rotterdam supplier of scoured Dutch wool and felt. The collective supplies makers of clothing, furniture and building products such as acoustic panels. Those products will get a passport in the coming years. Hollands Wol Collectief gets no passport duty of its own. Its customers' new duties reach the collective through information requests, and a supplier that wants to keep those customers has to be able to answer them.

The project ran in four steps. First an extensive literature study across several legal regimes, backed by documents and interviews from the field and a review of pilot projects. Then a data matrix for the collective's wool, listing every information requirement that is needed, expected, possible or desirable. That matrix was the basis for a maturity score for the collective's data management. Finally a position paper on a European wool passport, bringing the lessons together for the wool sector as a whole. One finding from it affects the entire sector: Europe's reference database for footprint calculations holds a single wool dataset, mostly Australian and New Zealand. European wool is scored on other farms' sheep.

Literature and the field

A passport belongs to a finished product. The preparatory study for textiles by the Joint Research Centre (JRC) puts the duty on whoever makes the coat or the rug, and leaves room for a later separate act or a voluntary track from the sector. A supplier of semi-finished goods usually falls outside it. Yet much of the data such a passport asks for starts with that supplier: origin, breed, scouring, treatments, substances.

Wool adds a twist. The same bale can end up in a jumper, a mattress or an acoustic panel. Then the rules for textiles, for furniture or for construction products apply, each with its own timetable and its own list of data points. A wool supplier gets questions from all three.

A Digital Product Passport is a digital record attached to a product through a QR code or chip. It tells you what the product is made of, how to repair it and what should happen to it at the end of its life. The Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation, ESPR (Regulation (EU) 2024/1781), sets out that such a passport will come. A delegated act per product group decides which data goes in. For textiles that act does not exist yet. There is a preparatory study, and furniture is in the working plan too. An overview of all product groups is on the page about Digital Product Passports.

Three duties can already apply to the collective itself. The declaration of conformity for packaging under the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation (PPWR), which has applied since 12 August 2026. The information duty under REACH Article 33 for any substance of very high concern above 0.1 percent by weight. And entry 77 of REACH Annex XVII, which has limited formaldehyde since 6 August 2026. What a supplier records for these duties is the evidence that later ends up in the customer's passport.

Beyond the rules, the study looked at practice. Regen Studio and CircularTech Forum spoke with a Dutch natural-fibre network, with a furniture maker that uses the wool as filling and with a French wool organisation. They also reviewed pilot projects, among them a European pilot with a passport for mattresses.

The data matrix

The textiles study, the rules for construction products, packaging and chemicals, and the furniture entry in the ESPR working plan together produced a matrix of 76 data points in eight categories. For each data point it records whether it is needed, expected, possible or desirable, and at which step of the chain it arises: from the farm and the scouring plant to the packaging and the end of life.

Maturity of the data management

On 30 June, on site and together with the collective, every data point was scored: present, partly present, absent, unknown or not applicable. Thirty data points were unknown and two did not apply. On the 44 the collective could answer, it scored 32 percent: seven were present, fourteen partly and twenty-three not, with partly present counting for half.

The thirty data points marked unknown are the most interesting. Nobody had ever asked the collective those questions. The answers sit with sheep farmers, scourers and felters. Most of the work on a passport therefore lies in conversations along the chain, long before any software comes in. On 9 July advice followed on how to collect and manage that data.

The position paper

The hardest problem lay outside the company. A footprint calculation under the European Environmental Footprint method falls back on the EF 3.1 reference database when a company has no numbers of its own. That database holds exactly one wool dataset, "global production mix, at farm". It describes mostly Australian and New Zealand production, with an undocumented split between wool and meat. For scouring, the default uses cotton as a proxy, while strong wool loses around forty percent of its weight to dirt in the wash, according to the project's data matrix.

Dutch wool is a residual stream. Here the fleece is worth almost nothing next to the meat. The dataset does not see that.

The choice that weighs heaviest is allocation: how the environmental burden of a sheep is split over wool, meat and milk. In published wool studies, compiled in the project's synthesis report, split by economic value, wool carries roughly 5 kg CO₂-equivalent per kilo. Split by protein mass, it carries 10 to 12, with outliers between 24 and 38. Those studies draw their system boundaries differently, so read these as orders of magnitude, not measured values. Which split the reference dataset uses is not documented. The method counts the sheep's methane and books no CO₂ uptake. And under the Product Environmental Footprint rules for apparel and footwear, durability only counts with test results: without tests the least favourable default applies.

Anyone without their own numbers gets the default. In the project's analysis that default comes out unfavourably for European wool, which makes for an unfair comparison for a material that arises here as a by-product. Split by economic value, Dutch wool would carry almost none of the sheep's burden, because the fleece earns little next to the meat. A global mix in which wool is a main product does not reflect that.

A company can replace the default with its own numbers: origin and breed per lot, and the energy, water and yield of scouring. A full dataset of its own asks a lot, though. It has to be built to the European method, documented in the EU's ILCD data format and checked by two independent reviewers. For most companies in the wool chain that is too heavy. In France a wool organisation and ADEME, the French environment agency, are building a dataset for French wool, due in the public French database at the end of 2026.

The position paper therefore argues that the problem can be solved once, for all member states together. The window is still open: the Product Environmental Footprint rules for apparel and footwear (version 3.1), which name this wool dataset, expire at the end of 2027, and the revision of the method had no published outcome by mid-2026.

So who should do that work? The project looked in all 27 member states, each in its own language, for organisations whose subject is wool. Six member states have one. In eleven the role is partly filled, in ten nobody was found. At European level, organisations work on textiles and fibres. No European wool body was found working on the passport question.

The proposal, worked out with the collective: work at European level on sector standards for the questions the product passport raises, with central coordination. One wool passport that works in all three regimes, so that everyone in the chain collects the data in the same place. A European data layer for wool is work for the sector as a whole, which should build and fund it together.

Lessons from the project

The first lesson is that a supplier without a passport duty of its own still holds the key. The data the customer will have to show starts largely with the supplier and earlier in the chain. Start with what is already there: weighbridge slips, purchase slips, the systems in use today.

The second lesson is about scale. A shared European dataset is something no single company fixes alone. For wool, the passport asks for cooperation between member states, on the system and on the language in which the data is recorded.

The third lesson is about how projects like this are set up. Make results transferable from the start. A data matrix, slides and a position paper that someone else can pick up are worth more than advice that lives inside one company.

Frequently asked questions

Does a wool supplier have to make its own product passport?

Most likely not. The preparatory study for textiles puts the passport duty on the finished product. A supplier of scoured wool or felt does supply much of the data that goes into the customer's passport.

Which duties can already apply to a wool supplier?

The declaration of conformity for packaging under the PPWR, since 12 August 2026. The information duty under REACH Article 33 for a substance of very high concern above 0.1 percent by weight. And entry 77 of REACH Annex XVII, which has limited formaldehyde since 6 August 2026.

Where does the missing passport data for wool sit?

Mostly earlier in the chain, with sheep farmers, scourers and felters. Thirty of the 76 data points in the matrix were unknown to the collective because nobody had asked for them before, so the work starts with conversations along the chain.

Why can the environmental calculation come out unfavourably for European wool?

The EF 3.1 reference database holds one wool dataset, mostly Australian and New Zealand. How the burden is split over wool, meat and milk can double the result, or in extreme cases multiply it by nearly eight. Anyone without their own numbers falls back on that default.

What do Regen Studio, CircularTech Forum and Hollands Wol Collectief propose?

Work at European level on sector standards for wool in the product passport, with central coordination, and one passport that works for textiles, furniture and construction products.
